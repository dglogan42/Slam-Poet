# Chapter Two: B at the Mic

Marlow's smelled like every good decision I'd ever made. Coffee, old paper, the particular dust of a bookstore that had been in the same building since before espresso machines existed, and, on Thursday nights, the ozone smell of a cheap PA system warming up in the corner by the poetry section.

I told my mother I was at Priya's. This was true in the sense that I did go to Priya's, first, for forty minutes, so that if anyone checked, Priya's mom could honestly say I'd been there, and then Priya drove me the eleven minutes to Marlow's in her ancient Corolla that smelled like the inside of a highlighter, and dropped me off, and picked me up two hours later, and never once asked me to explain the part in between beyond the first time, freshman year, when she'd asked exactly once and I'd said *I need this to be mine* and she'd said *okay* and never asked again. I loved Priya the specific way you love someone who respects a door you've closed without needing to know what's behind it.

Marlow's ran open mic sign-up on a clipboard by the register, and you could sign up under any name you wanted, because the woman who ran the night — Odette, fifties, silver buzzcut, reading glasses on a beaded chain, the kind of unbothered that took decades to build — had decided years ago that first names were a courtesy and stage names were a right. I always signed up as B.

Just the letter. Not short for anything, as far as anyone at Marlow's knew, though of course it was short for something, the same thing it had always been short for, but B felt like enough of a costume to let the rest of me stay backstage.

I'd found Marlow's by accident, technically, freshman year, ducking in out of a sudden October downpour with twenty minutes to kill before Priya could pick me up, and I'd wandered to the back out of pure curiosity, drawn by the sound of somebody's voice doing something no voice at Port Alden High ever did — reading like the words actually cost something to say. I hadn't planned to sign up that first night. I'd planned to sit in the back and drink a hot chocolate and wait out the rain. But Odette had come around with the clipboard anyway, offering it to everyone in the room without discrimination, regulars and strangers alike, and something about being handed a blank line with no explanation required had felt, that rainy Thursday, like being handed a door with no lock on it. I'd written B before I'd even decided to, the letter arriving in my hand faster than the thought behind it, and I'd gone up four names later and read the only poem I had memorized well enough to survive the terror, an early, clumsy thing about my Lolo's photo behind the register, and the applause afterward had done something to my chest that I spent the following two years chasing, every single Thursday, without ever quite admitting to myself that it had become the thing I looked forward to most in my entire week.

I got there at 7:10. The sign-up sheet already had six names on it — a college kid who did long, looping stuff about his ex-girlfriend that I privately thought needed an editor; a middle-aged man named Gary who read the same poem about his dog every single week and somehow made it land every single time; two Port Alden High kids I recognized from the hallway but had never spoken to, who clearly recognized me too and, in the specific etiquette of the open mic, pretended not to, the same way I pretended not to recognize them. It was an unspoken rule. Whatever we were on a Thursday night didn't travel back to Monday.

I wrote **B** on the seventh line and got a coffee I didn't need and found my usual chair, second row, aisle side, close enough to the little stage to see people's faces and far enough back that I didn't have to be the first thing anyone looked at when they walked in.

That was when I noticed the counter had a new person behind it.

Not new-new — I'd seen him before, vaguely, restocking the poetry shelves once in August, tall in the way that looked accidental, like he'd grown that way without particularly choosing to, dark hair that needed a haircut in the unbothered way that meant either he hadn't noticed or didn't care, which were different things and I found myself curious which one it was. He was making a latte with the kind of concentration usually reserved for bomb disposal.

"You're new," I said, when I went up for a refill, which was a stupid thing to say to someone who obviously worked there, but it came out before I filtered it, which almost never happened to me, and I felt my ears go warm.

"Three weeks," he said. "Which around here, from what I can tell, still counts as new. Gary's been coming for four years and Odette still calls him 'the new fellow with the dog poem.'"

"It's a good dog poem."

"It's the same dog poem."

"That's what makes it good," I said. "He's not performing it. He's just telling you the truth about his dog, every week, and it happens to rhyme."

He looked at me for a second longer than the transaction required, like I'd said something that surprised him into actually looking, and I felt that same warmth creep from my ears down into my collar. "That's a generous way to think about it."

"I have a lot of theories about the dog poem."

"Do you sign up?" He nodded at the clipboard. "I've seen your name. B. That short for something?"

"Everyone's name is short for something," I said, which was not an answer, and we both seemed to understand that it wasn't an answer, and he let it go with something that wasn't quite a smile but was clearly related to one.

"Eli," he said. "Not short for anything. My parents just really liked the sound of it, apparently."

"Bea," I said, and then heard myself say it, my actual name, out loud, to a person at the open mic, for the first time in two years of Thursdays, and something in my chest did a small, alarmed flip, like I'd leaned too far over a railing. "I mean. That's — you can call me B. Everyone here calls me B."

"Bea," he repeated, like he was trying it on, not pushing, just noting that he'd have it if he wanted it. "Okay."

He didn't make a thing of it. That was, I would understand much later, the first true fact I learned about Eli Cho: he had an exceptional talent for not making things into a bigger deal than the person offering them wanted them to be, which was rarer than it should have been, and which made him, over the following weeks, dangerously easy to tell things to.

Odette started the mic around 7:30 with her usual speech about phones on silent and applause instead of snapping because, quote, "we're not beatniks, we're a bookstore in a strip mall," and the night moved through Gary's dog poem (good, as advertised) and the college kid's ex-girlfriend material (still needed an editor) and one of the Port Alden kids doing something about basketball that was better than I expected, and then it was my turn, and Odette said, "Next up, we've got B," and I stood, and my legs did the thing they always did, going slightly unfamiliar under me, like they belonged to someone auditioning for the part of me.

Onstage was the only place in my life where the order reversed. Everywhere else I was quiet on the outside with a riot going on behind my face, exactly the way Lola had said. Up there, under the one clip light Marlow's owned, I got to be loud on the outside for three minutes and let the quiet stay backstage instead.

I did the diner poem. Not the version I'd been fighting with on the page all week — I'd found the fix that afternoon, sitting in the back of AP Bio while Mr. Alvarez explained mitosis, when the actual shape of the thing suddenly arrived whole, the way it sometimes did, like it had been finished the whole time and I'd just been waiting for it to let me catch up.

*The diner keeps the lights on late.
Nobody asks what the lights are for.
Everybody just wants the light.
My mother counts the register twice
because the first count is always
what she wishes were true
and the second count is what's actually there,
and she has spent twenty years
learning to love the second number
better than the first.
I am the second number.
I am dependable. I am correct.
I have never once
in my whole life
been anyone's wish.*

The room did the thing a good room does after a poem that lands — not applause right away, a half-second of held breath first, which is worth more than any clapping, because it means you put something into the air that people had to finish swallowing before they could respond. Then the applause came, real, and Gary said "there it is" to nobody in particular, and I sat back down with my heart going like I'd run somewhere, which I hadn't, except in the sense that I had, every single Thursday, for two years, been running toward the one place I got to say true things out loud.

Eli was watching from behind the counter when I looked over, not clapping because his hands were full of someone's order, but watching with an expression I didn't have a name for yet and would spend the next several chapters of my life learning to read.

After, packing up, he caught me by the door with two coffees, one of which he held out like it wasn't a big deal, which it also wasn't, except it was.

"That's not a fake name situation," he said. "That's a real thing you made."

"It's just a poem."

"It's not just anything." He said it simply, no performance in it, which was somehow more unsettling than if he'd tried to be smooth about it. "You should sign up as your real name sometime. Not for anyone else. Just — see how it feels."

"Maybe," I said, which was a lie, or at least a placeholder for a lie, because even then, standing in the doorway of the one place I got to be entirely myself, I could not picture a version of my life where those two names — B and Bea — occupied the same room without something in my house cracking open.

I didn't know yet how right I was. I also didn't know, standing there with a coffee I hadn't asked for going cold in my hand, that the crack had already started, three weeks earlier, the day Priya first mentioned a flyer on the school announcement board that I hadn't yet let myself really look at.
