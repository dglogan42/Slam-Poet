# Chapter Three: Sunday Call

Sunday calls happened at 7pm sharp, which was 6am Monday in Dubai, which meant my father was calling us from a labor camp dormitory before his shift, sitting on the edge of a bunk with four other men still asleep behind him, keeping his voice low and his face bright, a combination I'd watched him perform every single Sunday for three years without ever once seeing the seams show.

We did it in the living room, all four of us, Nico usually already in his pajamas because Sunday nights he got to stay up twenty extra minutes if the call ran long, which it never did, because international data was expensive and my father was the kind of man who apologized for a bad connection like it was a personal failing.

"There she is," he said, when my face filled the frame, the tablet propped against the fruit bowl at an angle that always made my chin look enormous. "Look how big. You cut your hair?"

"Six months ago, Papa."

"I'm seeing it fresh every time. That's the beauty of the time difference." He laughed at his own joke, which he made some version of nearly every week, and I laughed too, because it was still funny, mostly because of how much he wanted it to be.

My father had a face built for video calls in a way I don't think he knew, wide and open, the kind of face that made a bad phone camera and worse lighting look almost incidental. He'd been in Dubai three years on his second contract, wiring electrical systems into buildings he described to us in scraps — the height of them, the strangeness of working thirty stories up with the whole Gulf laid out under him like a map, the cold of the air conditioning that never quite matched the heat outside, the men from Kerala and Manila and Lagos he worked beside who'd become, in his descriptions, almost a second family, one I'd never meet and had learned everything about secondhand: Sanjay who could fix anything, Emeka who sent his kids to a school with a pool, old Tatay Ben who'd been doing this for twenty years and swore every single contract was his last.

"Nico," my father said, "show me your cards."

Nico, who had been vibrating with the specific patience of a ten-year-old waiting his turn, scrambled up onto the couch arm and held his newest Kaiju Clash card up to the camera at a distance that made it entirely unreadable, a habit my father never corrected because correcting it would have meant less time looking at Nico's face and more time looking at a card, and my father had priorities.

"Powerful," my father said solemnly. "What does it do?"

"It's a legendary, Papa, it does eight hundred damage but only if you already played a water type—"

He explained for four straight minutes. My father listened to every word like it was market data, nodding, asking one good follow-up question that proved he'd actually tracked it, and I watched my little brother's whole body ease into the specific shape of a kid who has just been fully, completely listened to, and felt something in my chest that I didn't examine too closely, because examining it always led to the same place, which was: *when's the last time somebody asked me a follow-up question about the thing I loved.*

Lola took her turn next, mostly business — had he been to the clinic about his shoulder, was he eating, had he called his sister in Cebu — delivered in a rapid-fire Tagalog my father answered in the same rhythm, the two of them falling into a cadence that always made me feel like I was watching a conversation from behind glass, close enough to catch the shape of it, not quite fluent enough to catch every word, which was its own small, specific loneliness I'd never told anyone about, not even Priya.

Then my mother took the tablet into the kitchen, the way she always did, for the part of the call that was theirs.

I didn't listen. Not because I couldn't have — the apartment wasn't big enough to make eavesdropping difficult — but because there was a version of privacy my family observed without ever discussing it, doors we didn't open even when they weren't locked. I helped Nico get his cards back into their binder, in the specific order he insisted on, water types first, and half-listened instead to the murmur of my mother's voice through the kitchen doorway, rising and falling, laughing once, going quiet for a stretch that meant they were talking about money, which they always eventually were, because that was the actual shape of my parents' marriage right now, three years of it: a relationship conducted mostly in numbers, wired and received, budgeted and stretched, love translated into remittances because the two of them had decided, before I was old enough to have a vote in it, that being apart and solvent was better than being together and drowning.

I didn't resent it. That was the strange thing I could never quite explain to Priya, who'd once asked, gently, if it made me sad, my dad being gone so much of my life, and I'd said the true thing, which was: not sad, exactly. Just — arithmetic. My father's absence had a shape I'd grown up around the way you grow up around a support beam in the middle of a room. You didn't resent the beam. You just always knew where it was, and you built the rest of the furniture around it.

I still remembered the day he left for the first contract, though I'd been thirteen, young enough that the memory had gone slightly soft at the edges the way memories do, more feeling than footage. The airport smelled like coffee and jet fuel. My father had knelt down in the security line to be eye-level with Nico, who was seven then and didn't understand the difference between a trip and a departure, and told him he was going to build the tallest building either of them had ever seen and send pictures every week, a promise he had, to his enormous credit, actually kept. He'd hugged my mother for so long that the woman behind them in line had started, gently, to clear her throat. And he'd turned to me last, the oldest, the one who was supposed to already understand things like this, and hadn't said anything at all for a second, just held my face in both his hands the way you'd hold something you were afraid of dropping, and said, *anak, you're going to have to be the strong one while I'm gone, okay? Not forever. Just for now.* I had said okay. I had been thirteen years old and I had said okay, and three years later I still hadn't found the moment to tell him that "for now" had turned out to mean something closer to forever than either of us had probably meant it to, back in that security line, back when three years still sounded like a number small enough to hold in your hand.

I didn't blame him for it. That was important to me, even now, even lying in bed turning the whole architecture of my family over in my head — none of this was a thing anyone had done *to* me. It was just the shape the math had taken, the specific arithmetic of a family that needed a wage strong enough to keep a lease and a mortgage and a ten-year-old's allowance all solvent at once, and Dubai paid better than anything Port Alden had to offer a man with my father's specific certifications. Somebody had run those numbers, once, at a kitchen table very much like the one two rooms away from me right now, and the numbers had said *go,* and my father had gone, and I had, without ever formally agreeing to it, inherited the job of holding down everything he'd left a father-shaped hole behind him to hold.

"Bea." My mother, back from the kitchen, held the tablet out. "Two minutes. Say goodnight."

My father's face again, tired now in the way it got by the end of a call, the brightness starting to cost him visibly. "How's school, anak?"

"Good. AP Bio's brutal. Everything else is fine."

"And the diner? You're helping your mother?"

"Every day."

"Good girl." He said it warmly, meaning it as the highest compliment he had, and I took it as one, mostly, the way I always did, filing the small, complicated feeling that came with it somewhere I didn't examine, the same drawer where I kept the feeling I got watching him ask Nico four straight minutes about a card game. "You're taking care of everyone."

"Somebody has to."

I meant it as a joke. It came out flatter than I meant it, and I saw something cross his face — not hurt, exactly, more like a man catching a glimpse of a bill he hadn't budgeted for — and I backpedaled fast, the way I always did when I said something too true out loud by accident.

"I'm kidding, Papa. It's fine. I like it."

"You're a good daughter," he said, and the call ended a minute later on schedule, the screen going dark on his tired, bright face, and the apartment doing the specific settle it always did after Sunday calls, everyone drifting back to their separate orbits, Nico to his cards, Lola to the news, my mother to the kitchen to start Monday's prep a night early because she never really stopped.

I went to my room and sat on my bed with my notebook and didn't write anything for a long time. I just sat with the sentence that had gotten stuck somewhere under my sternum since the call ended.

*Somebody has to.*

It wasn't even a lie. That was what made it sit so strangely — I did like it, most days, the specific competence of being the person a family could set its weight on without the floor giving way. I liked being good at it. I think I had built a fairly large percentage of my whole personality on being good at it.

I just, some nights, lying in the dark with the sound of my mother's knife going through vegetables two rooms away, found myself wondering what would happen to all of us if I ever once said the other true sentence instead. Not *somebody has to.*

Just: *I want something, too, and it isn't this.*

I didn't write that down either. I was sixteen, and there are some sentences you have to carry around unwritten for a while before you're ready to see them on a page, in your own handwriting, undeniable.

Nico knocked on my door frame a while later, still in his pajamas, holding his binder against his chest like a shield. "Can I show you the legendary one? Papa didn't really get to see the back, where the stats are."

"Sure," I said, and made room on the bed, and he climbed up and opened to the exact page, and I looked at the stats block on the back of a cardboard rectangle with the same careful attention my father had given the front of it twenty minutes earlier, because that was the deal in our house, the one nobody had ever written down: whatever Papa couldn't finish from four thousand miles away, someone closer had to pick up before it hit the ground. "Eight hundred damage is a lot," I said. "That's basically the whole game, right?"

"Only if I already played a water type first. That's the trade-off." He said *trade-off* like he'd learned the phrase recently and was proud of having a use for it, which he probably had. "Do you think Papa's building is really the tallest one he's ever seen?"

"I think he tells you that because he likes how your face looks when he says it."

Nico considered this with the seriousness of a much older person. "That's a nice reason to say something that might not be true."

"Yeah," I said. "It is." And I thought, not for the first time, that my little brother understood more about how our family worked than any of us gave him credit for, and I pulled him in against my side and let him talk about water types until he got sleepy enough to be sent to bed without protest, which took, as it usually did, almost exactly nineteen of his twenty allotted extra minutes.

Downstairs, faintly, I heard the register drawer — Lola, doing an unnecessary check of a total she'd already checked twice that morning, because some nights the numbers were the only thing in the house that would sit still and be exactly what she needed them to be.
