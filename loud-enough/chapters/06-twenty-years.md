# Chapter Six: Twenty Years

My mother called a family meeting, which in our house meant everyone had to sit at the same booth at the same time with no phones, an event rare enough that Nico asked, with real alarm, if someone had died.

"Nobody died," my mother said. "Sit."

It was Sunday afternoon, before the call to my father, the diner closed for the two hours between lunch and dinner prep that my mother guarded like a border. She'd made a folder. My mother making a folder for something was a signal on the level of a weather alert; the last folder had been for Nico's school enrollment, and the one before that had been for the diner's health inspection renewal, which meant folders in my family were reserved for things that mattered enough to need paper.

"October twenty-fourth," she said, sliding a paper across the table like she was dealing cards. "Twenty years since your Lolo and I signed the lease. I want to do something. Not just a discount on pancit. A real night."

Lola, next to her, was nodding along like this was a conversation they'd already had, which it clearly was, the two of them a united front with the specific energy of people who'd stayed up late at the kitchen table with a legal pad.

"A real night how," I said.

"Extended hours. A menu — some of the old dishes we don't do anymore, the ones your Lolo used to make. Live music, maybe, somebody's cousin plays guitar. I want to invite the paper. I want the whole neighborhood in that door, not just the regulars." My mother's face had the bright-and-tired thing again, more bright than tired this time, an idea she'd clearly been carrying around and polishing for weeks finally getting its debut. "Twenty years is not nothing, Bea. Your Lolo and I opened that door with eleven thousand dollars and a five-year lease we could barely afford, and here we are."

"It's a beautiful idea," I said, and meant it, watching her, because it was — you could see the whole shape of it in her face, the diner packed and loud and lit up, my grandfather's photo behind the register finally getting the kind of night he'd have loved. "What do you need from us?"

"Everything," Lola said, which made Nico laugh, and even my mother cracked a smile.

"What old dishes?" Nico asked, already angling for the part of this that involved food, which was, with Nico, always the fastest route into any conversation.

"The ones your Lolo made when we first opened," Lola said, and her whole face changed the way it always did around my grandfather, something in it going soft and far away at once. "Kaldereta with real tripe, not the shortcut version I make now. A rice cake he learned from his own mother, back in Cebu, that takes two days to do right because you have to let the batter sour properly, no shortcuts, no substitutions, or it comes out flat and sweet instead of something with a little bite to it. He used to say any restaurant that skips that step doesn't deserve to serve it at all." She said this like a rule of law, absolute, unnegotiable, and I understood that whatever came out of that kitchen on October twenty-fourth was going to be soured exactly right, because Lola would sit up two nights running to make sure of it if she had to. "Your mother has never made it herself. I always did that part."

"I'll learn it," my mother said, and there was something almost nervous in how quickly she said it, like she'd been waiting a long time for permission to ask.

"I'll teach you," Lola said, and reached over and patted my mother's hand once, businesslike, already moving past the moment before either of them could get too sentimental about it in front of the children, though I saw it anyway, the whole shape of what had just passed between them — a recipe was never just a recipe in this family, it was a thing you were trusted to carry once somebody decided you were ready to carry it.

The plan, once it unspooled across the table, was enormous in the specific way my mother's plans always were — she thought in "whole neighborhood," never in "manageable pieces," which was, I'd come to understand over sixteen years, both the reason Sampaguita had survived a recession, two lease renewals, and one very bad year when the health inspector found a problem with the walk-in, and also the reason every big event in our family's life arrived like weather, something you didn't so much plan for as brace against. Extended hours meant extra shifts, which meant extra staffing, which — since my mother didn't trust hired help with anything that mattered, a philosophy that had only calcified since my father left — meant family.

Nico would run drinks and bussing, supervised. Lola would cook the old dishes, the ones she'd been promising to teach my mother for years and never quite gotten around to, which meant this was also, quietly, going to be a kind of transfer, Lola's hands doing something my mother's hands would need to learn to do without her someday, a thought nobody said out loud but that sat in the room anyway, the way certain thoughts do in families who've already lost one person too early to pretend time doesn't move. My mother would run the kitchen and the front, as always, doubled.

And I would run everything else. The register, the overflow, the reservations she wanted to start taking for the first time ever, the flyers she wanted printed and posted, the neighbors she wanted personally invited, the follow-up with the newspaper contact she'd gotten from a regular whose daughter worked there.

"You're good at this," my mother said, which was true, and which she said the way you'd say *you're good at math* to someone, a simple observation of fact, not noticing — she never noticed, it wasn't unkindness, it was just how she was built — that being told you're good at something is not always the same as being asked if you want to do it. "You'll be the one people call if there's a question. You always are."

"What's the date again?" I asked, and I already knew, somehow, from the small cold drop in my stomach before she even answered, exactly what she was going to say.

"Saturday, October twenty-fourth."

I did the math I'd already half-done two weeks ago without letting myself finish it. The regional heat was October third — three weeks before the anniversary, no conflict there, that Saturday was still, technically, mine. But State Voices' website, which I'd checked exactly once, late at night, in the specific furtive way I did everything related to this now, had listed the finals date in small gray text under the regional heat information, a date I'd read and then made myself stop thinking about because thinking about it meant thinking about winning, and I hadn't let myself want that yet, not out loud, not even to myself.

**State Finals: Saturday, November 7.**

Not October twenty-fourth. I checked the math twice at the table, relief loosening something in my chest before I'd even fully understood why I needed it to. The dates didn't collide. I had — if I won, if I even got that far, which felt like an enormous and possibly arrogant *if* — a full two weeks of breathing room between the anniversary and the finals.

I let myself relax into the rest of the meeting, nodding at flyers and reservation books and the guest list Lola was already drafting from memory, half in English and half in Tagalog, forty years of customers filed away in her head like she had her own ledger nobody else could read. Nico asked if there'd be balloons. My mother said maybe. Nico said definitely, in the specific tone of a ten-year-old already mentally spending a hypothetical yes as a guarantee, and then spent the next ten minutes negotiating colors — gold and white, he decided, because gold was for twenty years being a big deal and white was because Lola liked things to match the string lights — with a seriousness usually reserved for treaties, and my mother let him have every single one of his demands, which told me, more than the folder or the legal pad or any of it, exactly how much this night meant to her: she was giving her ten-year-old veto power over party decor, and my mother did not give up control of anything easily.

It was only later, doing the dishes after the meeting broke up, that the second half of the arithmetic caught up with me, the part I hadn't let myself do at the table because I'd been too relieved by the first part.

The regional heat was October third. If — *if* — I won, that put me into the finals on November seventh. But the finals were three hours away, in the state capital, an all-day event according to the website, registration at nine, awards not until six. My mother's twenty-years plan didn't end on October twenty-fourth. It was the launch of something, I understood now, turning the plate in my hands under water that had gone lukewarm — she'd said it herself, hadn't she, buried in the excitement of the folder: *I want the whole neighborhood in that door, not just the regulars.* She wasn't planning one big night. She was planning a season. Extended hours "for a few weeks after, to keep the momentum going, while people are still talking about it." She'd said that too, and I'd nodded along with everyone else, filing it under *Mom's enthusiasm, we'll see how much of it survives contact with actual Tuesdays.*

November seventh might not be a single date I needed to defend. It might be a Saturday in the middle of the exact stretch of weeks my mother was already planning to need me most.

I didn't say anything that night. I dried the plate and put it away and went and sat on my bed with the notebook, and I didn't write a poem, I just sat with the calendar app open on my phone, October third circled, November seventh circled, twenty-four days between them that I had, three hours earlier, believed were going to be enough room to have both things.

I was starting to understand that in my family, there was no such thing as enough room. There was only ever the amount of room my mother's dreams needed, and whatever was left over after that, which up until now had always, without my ever quite deciding it that way, been the amount of room I gave myself.
