# Chapter Four: The Flyer

Priya found the flyer on the announcement board outside the guidance office, which was where all of Port Alden High's most consequential paper lived, sandwiched between a lost-cat notice that had been up so long the cat had probably died of old age and a sign-up for a canned food drive that ended, according to the date, two months ago.

"Bea." She grabbed my sleeve so hard I nearly walked into the trophy case. "Bea, look."

I looked. **STATE VOICES — TEEN POETRY SLAM**, in a font that was trying too hard, over a photo of a kid mid-poem with their mouth open and their hand a blur. **Regional Heat: Port Alden Branch Library, Sat. Oct. 3. Winner advances to State Finals, Nov. 7, State Capital. Cash prize + publication in State Voices anthology.** A QR code. A deadline for registration two weeks out.

"So?" I said, already walking, because I had exactly nine minutes to get from the second floor to the science wing and Mr. Alvarez took points off for tardiness like it was a moral failing.

"So." Priya kept pace with me, which took effort, because Priya was five-two and I was not, and she'd been matching my stride since seventh grade out of pure stubbornness. "So this is literally the thing. This is the exact thing you do every single Thursday in a bookstore that maybe forty people total have ever heard you in."

"I don't do this."

"You write poems and you read them out loud to strangers for applause, Bea, I don't know what to tell you, that is a slam, that is the actual definition—"

"It's different."

"How."

I didn't have a good answer ready, which was unusual for me, because I was generally excellent at having good answers ready, it was practically my whole personality outside the diner, and the gap where the answer should have been sat there long enough that Priya noticed it too.

Priya had known about the notebook since ninth grade, the year she'd caught me writing in it during lunch and asked, with the same directness she brought to everything, whether it was a diary, and I'd said no, and she'd said, okay, then what, and I'd shown her exactly one poem — a bad one, about the diner's grease trap, of all things, that had nonetheless made her go quiet in a way that told me she'd understood something true about it even through the bad metaphor — and that had been enough, apparently, to earn her a permanent, patient interest in a part of my life she'd never once tried to force open further than I let her.

"You know what I think it actually is?" she said now, matching my stride down the hall even though the bell was going to ring on both of us if she didn't peel off soon. "I think Marlow's is safe because it's borrowed. You get to be a poet there for exactly two hours a week and then you give the name back, like a costume from a rental shop, and nobody has to know you kept wearing it the other six days." She said it without any judgment in it, which somehow made it land harder than if she'd been needling me. "A slam with your real name on it, you don't get to give it back. You just get to be someone who writes poems, full time, forever, whether or not you're standing on a stage."

"It's different," I said again, "because at Marlow's nobody knows me. If I stand up in a library on a Saturday with my actual name on a program, that's not the same thing. That's not — I don't do that."

"Why not?"

We'd reached the science wing. I had four minutes now, and Priya had a free period, and the specific injustice of that — that she got to keep having this conversation while I had to go learn about mitochondria — irritated me more than the conversation itself.

"Because it's not what I do," I said, which wasn't an answer either, and Priya knew it wasn't an answer, and she let me go into class with the flyer's photo still bright behind my eyes, the kid with their mouth open and their hand a blur, looking like they'd forgotten, for the length of the photograph, that anyone else existed at all.

I thought about it through the entire mitochondria unit, which Mr. Alvarez would not have appreciated.

Here was the true shape of the problem, the one I hadn't said out loud to Priya because saying it out loud made it real: Marlow's worked because it cost nothing. If I stood up on a Thursday and the poem didn't land, if my voice cracked, if I forgot a line — none of that traveled. Odette wouldn't tell my mother. Gary wasn't going to mention it at the diner. B was a name with no address, no family, no eleven-year history of being the reliable one; B could fail in a bookstore forty people deep and walk home unscathed, because B didn't have to be anything the next morning except Bea again, quiet, correct, the second number.

A regional heat at the public library, under my actual name, with a program and a photographer from the local paper who covered these things — I knew, because the paper covered everything in Port Alden that involved a folding table and more than six attendees — was not a costless place to fail. It was not even a costless place to *succeed,* because succeeding would mean something had to be explained afterward, to my mother, who would want to know when this had started and why she hadn't known, and Lola, who noticed everything and said nothing until she decided it was time, and to my father, four thousand miles away on a call I could already imagine going strange and careful, his voice doing the thing it did when he was recalibrating an idea of me he'd been carrying since I was small.

I didn't want to explain it. I wanted, if I was honest — and lunch that day found me being uncomfortably honest with myself, alone at the end of the table while Priya argued a debate case out loud at the person next to her, gesturing with a fry — I wanted to just *do* it, quietly, and see what happened, and then decide afterward whether it needed explaining at all.

Which was, I would understand much later, the actual shape of every decision I made for the next six weeks: not *should I do this,* but *can I do this without anyone finding out until I've already decided how I feel about it.*

Priya sat down across from me with her tray, done arguing, and looked at me with the specific patience of someone who has decided to let a point rest for exactly as long as it needs to and not one second longer.

"You don't have to sign up," she said. "I'm not going to bug you about it."

"You bugged me about it for four straight minutes in the hallway."

"That was the allotted bugging period. It's over now." She stole one of my fries, which was also an allotted ritual, one I'd never once actually minded. "I just think — " She stopped, reconsidered, started again in the careful voice she used maybe three times a year, when she meant something enough to risk it landing wrong. "I think you're the best writer I know, and I've watched you hide it for two years like it's something you're embarrassed of, and I don't actually think you're embarrassed of the poems. I think you're embarrassed of wanting something. Which is different."

I didn't say anything. The cafeteria did its cafeteria noise around us, trays and someone's laugh three tables over and the specific echo of a room built for acoustic problems nobody had solved since 1994.

"That's not fair," I said finally, which wasn't a denial, and Priya, who had known me since we were eleven years old and had learned to hear the difference between my denials and my non-denials with the precision of a bomb technician, just nodded like I'd confirmed something.

"I know," she said. "I still think it's true."

She let it go after that, genuinely, the way she always did, moving on to whether Ms. Reyes was going to assign the debate topic she'd threatened or a different one, and I ate my lunch and let my brain keep circling the flyer, the QR code, the deadline two weeks out, the photo of the kid with their mouth open like they'd forgotten anyone else existed.

That night, closing shift, I found myself watching my mother count the register — first count, second count, the small crease between her eyebrows easing once the numbers agreed with each other — and thought about what Priya had said, that I wasn't embarrassed of the poems, I was embarrassed of wanting something.

I turned it over while I mopped. It didn't feel entirely true. It also didn't feel entirely false, which was somehow worse, because a clean lie you could argue with, and a half-true thing just sat in your chest being uncomfortable and correct.

I didn't sign up that night. I told myself I was still deciding.

I was lying to myself the same easy way I lied to my mother about Priya's house, the same easy way I'd been lying about a dozen small things for two years — not because I was a liar, I didn't think, but because some truths in my house needed a running start before you could say them out loud, and I hadn't built up enough speed yet.

I had, though, without quite admitting it to myself, already memorized the deadline. Two weeks. I did the math without meaning to, the way you do math on a number that matters more than you're ready to say: two weeks from the flyer put registration closing on a Friday, which put the regional heat itself on the same Saturday the diner's supply order always came in early, which meant — if I was doing this, if, hypothetically, in some version of my life where I said yes to something that was only mine — I would need exactly four hours on an October Saturday that my mother had no reason yet to know I needed.

Four hours. It didn't seem like very much to ask.

I did not yet understand how much four hours could cost, or that the real number was never going to be the hours at all.
