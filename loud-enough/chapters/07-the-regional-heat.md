# Chapter Seven: The Regional Heat

I told my mother I was spending Saturday at Priya's, studying for a history test that did, in fact, exist, three weeks out, which meant the lie had a kind of scaffolding of truth around it that made it easier to say without my voice doing anything suspicious. Priya drove. Priya always drove, and it occurred to me somewhere around the second week of this arrangement that I had never once thanked her properly for being a getaway car with a soul, and I made a note to fix that, filed it in the same drawer where I kept most of my good intentions toward people who deserved better than what I gave them in the moment.

The Port Alden Branch Library had converted its community room into something that almost looked like a real venue — folding chairs in rows, a small stage with actual stage lighting someone had clearly borrowed from the community theater down the street, a table by the door with name tags and a sign-up sheet and a woman with a clipboard who looked at my registration and said, "Beatrice Santos?" like she was confirming a delivery.

"Bea," I said, and she wrote **BEA S.** on a name tag in blue marker and handed it to me, and I stood there for a second holding it, my actual first name on an adhesive strip, no letter standing in for it, no costume, and felt the particular vertigo of a door I'd been circling for two years finally swinging open whether I was ready or not.

Twenty-two competitors signed up in the youth category. I recognized nobody, which helped, and also nobody recognized me, which helped more — Port Alden was a decent-sized city, big enough that its poetry kids and its Sampaguita-eating families didn't automatically overlap, and I clung to that anonymity like a hand rail going down a dark staircase.

Backstage, if you could call a converted storage room backstage, the other competitors did the specific pre-performance choreography I recognized from two years of Thursdays, scaled up — some pacing with earbuds in, mouthing their pieces silently; some doing the deliberately loose, shaken-out arm-swings people do when they've read one article too many about stage nerves; one boy sitting perfectly still in a folding chair with his eyes closed, lips moving, so still that I almost didn't clock him as nervous at all until I noticed his knee going like a sewing machine under his hand. I recognized every single one of these rituals in myself, and it helped, oddly, to see them mirrored in strangers — a whole room of people who had each decided, independently, that this particular kind of terror was worth walking toward on purpose.

A girl next to me, dark curls, a denim jacket covered in patches, caught me staring at the name tag table and said, without preamble, "First time?" When I nodded, she said, "Third for me. You get less scared and more excited, it's a weirdly good trade," and squeezed my arm once before her name got called for the sign-in line, a small unprompted kindness from a total stranger that I carried with me, still slightly stunned by it, all the way to my own seat in the folding chairs.

Priya sat in the third row with her phone camera ready and an expression of such fierce, undisguised pride that I had to look away from her twice before my own turn just to keep my composure. She had brought, without asking me, a poster board sign that said **GO B** in glitter marker, which she'd had the mercy to keep folded in her bag until after I performed, because she understood, without my needing to explain it, that walking in with a sign bearing my slam name would have blown open a door I wasn't ready to walk through yet, even here, even now.

The format was three rounds, five judges pulled from the audience, scores on a scale from one to ten, top three from each round advancing. I performed the diner poem in round one — the same one I'd debuted at Marlow's weeks earlier, polished now, the metaphor trimmed of its excess coat until it fit close to the skin the way I wanted — and got an 8.6 average, high enough to feel the particular lightness of a thing landing exactly where you aimed it.

Round two I did a new piece, one I'd been working on since the Sunday call, about my father's voice through a bad connection, the specific loneliness of loving someone across a screen that occasionally froze him mid-sentence with his mouth still open, a small glitchy purgatory that felt, some weeks, like the truest image I had for what our family actually was. I hadn't performed it anywhere before this. My hands shook through the first two lines and then steadied, the way they always did once the poem remembered it was bigger than my nerves.

*My father freezes sometimes,
mid-word, mid-love,
the wifi in a labor camp dormitory
not built for tenderness at this distance,
and I have learned to wait out the freeze
the way you wait out weather,
knowing the sentence is still coming,
knowing love doesn't need
a stable connection
so much as it needs
someone willing to wait
on the other end
of a frozen screen
until the picture moves again.*

The room went very quiet after that one, the good kind of quiet, and a woman two rows back who I didn't know wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand in a way that made something in my own chest crack open a little, because I hadn't expected a stranger to feel the exact shape of my specific, private ache. That was the thing about the poems I hadn't fully understood until that afternoon, standing under borrowed stage lighting in a library community room: the more precisely I described the thing that was only mine, the more it turned out to belong, a little, to everyone else too.

I made finals with a 9.1 average across two rounds, which put me third overall, which meant a third-round performance for the top three, winner-take-the-slot, the other two finalists being a boy named Marcus who did blistering, furious spoken word about his brother's incarceration that left half the room stunned silent, and a girl named Wren who performed something quieter and stranger about her body that I thought, honestly, might be the best poem I heard all day.

I did the third poem I'd prepared, the riskiest one, about Lola's sampaguita flowers in the window box, the ones she watered every morning before the diner opened even though sampaguita didn't really thrive in Port Alden's climate, wasn't built for this cold, this rain, this wrong hemisphere of light — and I let the poem be about that, about things that got carried somewhere they weren't built for and had to figure out, stubbornly, season after season, how to bloom anyway.

I won. Not by much — the scores when they read them out had me point-two ahead of Wren, close enough that I felt almost guilty about it, and Wren found me afterward and hugged me, genuinely, no performance in it, and said, "Your grandmother poem wrecked me, I hope you know that," which I would carry around for a long time afterward, a small warm stone in my pocket.

The woman with the clipboard handed me a folder — **STATE VOICES FINALS, NOVEMBER 7TH, STATE CAPITAL** printed across the front in the same too-hard-trying font from the flyer — and inside it a confirmation, a schedule, a map of the venue, a media release form I'd have to get a parent to sign.

I stood in the library parking lot afterward with Priya, who had, at this point, entirely abandoned any pretense of composure and was holding the folded **GO B** sign against her chest like she was hugging it, and I felt, for one entire uninterrupted minute, purely and simply happy, no arithmetic running underneath it, no drawer in the back of my mind where the complicated feelings went to wait. Just happy. My name — my actual name — on a folder that said I'd been the best poet in a room of twenty-two people, on a Saturday I'd stolen for myself and gotten away with.

Then Priya said, gently, the way you'd approach a dog that had just been startled, "So. The form."

"What form."

"The one that says *media release, must be signed by parent or guardian.*" She nodded at the folder in my hands. "Bea. You're going to have to tell somebody something."

The happiness didn't disappear exactly. It just made room, the way it always did in my life, for the other thing sitting right behind it, patient, familiar, already sharpening its questions: *when. how. and what, exactly, happens to the room I've built for myself once the people I love the most finally get a key to it.*

I looked at the form. A parent or guardian signature, printed name, date. A single line, black ink, nothing complicated about it structurally — and somehow the hardest four inches of paper I had ever been asked to fill out in my life.

"I'll figure it out," I said, which was what I always said, and Priya, mercifully, let me have that lie for one more day, and drove me home past the diner, its windows lit gold in the early dark, my mother's silhouette visible through the glass doing the thing she always did at this hour, moving between the register and the kitchen and the booth where Lola sat with her ledger, three generations keeping a small light burning on a street where, twenty-four days from now, they intended to throw the door wide open and let the whole neighborhood in — with no idea yet that their own daughter had just quietly, secretly, become someone worth letting in, too.
