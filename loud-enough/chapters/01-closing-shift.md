# Chapter One: Closing Shift

The last ticket of the night was a to-go order for pancit and two lumpia, and I already knew before I looked up that it was going to be Mr. Delgado, because Mr. Delgado came in at 8:40 every Tuesday like the diner owed him something, which I guess in a way it did, since he'd been eating here since before I was born.

"Bea," he said, like he was surprised to see me, though I'd rung him up every Tuesday since June. "You still here? Shouldn't you be out doing teenager things?"

"This is a teenager thing," I said, and punched his order into the register, which shuddered on every third keystroke like it had a grudge against the number seven. "This is basically a hobby."

He laughed the wheezy laugh he had, the one that turned into a cough halfway through, and told me to say hi to my mother, and I said I would, even though my mother was eleven feet away scraping down the flat-top and had definitely heard him, because Sampaguita Diner was not a big place. It was booths and a counter and a kitchen you could see into if you leaned, string lights around the window that Lola strung up for Christmas six years ago and nobody had ever taken down, and a bell over the door that had rung so many times it had started to sound less like a bell and more like a fact of physics.

I knew this room better than I knew my own bedroom, honestly — knew which booth's vinyl seat had a slow leak that needed a strip of duct tape reapplied every few months, knew the register's seven-key grudge, knew exactly how many steps it took to get from the counter to the walk-in without clipping the corner of the third booth, a route I could run blind, and some nights, closing up alone with just Lola's radio for company, I basically did. There was a version of my life, I sometimes thought, where I could have resented all of it — the hours, the smell of grease that never fully washed out of my hair, the fact that other kids at Port Alden High spent their Tuesday nights at football practice or on couches watching shows I'd never seen a single episode of. But resentment had never quite taken hold in me the way I think people expected it to, watching a sixteen-year-old run a register after school. Mostly what I felt, closing up Sampaguita, was something closer to ownership. This room existed, at least in part, because of hours I had put into it. That had to count for something, even on the nights it also felt like it was the only thing that counted.

Mr. Delgado left. I flipped the sign. Closing shift had a rhythm to it that I liked better than I would ever admit out loud: chairs up on tables, the mop bucket's particular smell of pine cleaner and old grease, the register drawer counted twice because Mom didn't trust the first count of anything, not the drawer, not the weather, not compliments. Lola was in the back booth doing what she called "the books," which meant she had a calculator, a ledger with a cracked spine, and a rosary looped twice around her wrist that she touched every time a number didn't add up the way she wanted it to.

"Anak," she said, not looking up. "Bring me the coffee."

"You'll be up till one."

"I am up till one anyway. Might as well be for a reason."

I brought her the coffee. She patted the booth seat next to her without looking away from the ledger, which was Lola for *sit*, and I sat, and for a second neither of us said anything, and the diner made its end-of-night sounds — the flat-top ticking as it cooled, the walk-in's compressor kicking on, my mother's radio in the kitchen playing an OPM station low enough that it was more vibration than song.

This was the part of the day I'd learned to love without meaning to. Not the customers, not even the food, though I'd defend Lola's pancit to the death. The part where the work was mostly done and what was left was just us, moving around each other in a space we'd built our whole lives inside, not needing to explain anything because there was nothing that needed explaining.

"You did the register wrong Thursday," Lola said. "Sixty cents short."

"I know. I told Mom."

"You always tell your mother first." She said it like an observation about the weather, not a criticism, but I felt it land somewhere under my ribs anyway. "You could tell me first sometime."

"You're both in the same building, Lola."

"That's not what I mean, and you know it's not what I mean." She finally looked up. Lola had the kind of face that had clearly spent seventy-one years deciding exactly how much of anything to show at any given time, and right then she was showing me patience, which from Lola was its own kind of pointed. "You have a face like your mother's. Everything going on behind it, nothing coming out the front."

"I have a face like a person who wants to finish the mop bucket."

"Mm," she said, which in Lola meant *I am choosing not to fight you about this tonight,* and went back to her ledger.

I finished the mop bucket. I wiped down the register area, and it was there, tucked under the counter where I kept my backpack, that my hand found the edge of my notebook and I felt the small electric flinch I always felt when I touched it in public — which the back of a closing diner technically was not, but which felt, to the part of my brain that ran this particular alarm system, exactly as exposed as the middle of the cafeteria.

It was a plain black notebook, the kind you buy in a three-pack at the drugstore, spine gone soft from being carried around, corners fuzzed from my backpack. I'd started it freshman year because my English teacher, Ms. Okafor, had made us keep "writer's notebooks" for a unit on poetry, and the unit had ended in October and I had, for reasons I still hadn't fully explained to myself, kept going.

Nobody in my family knew it existed. Not because I thought they'd forbid it — my mother wasn't the forbidding type, she was the *busy* type, which could look similar from a distance but wasn't — but because there was no good moment to bring it up. You didn't casually mention, over a dinner where everyone was already tired, *hey, also, I write poems, and also I read them out loud in front of strangers on Thursdays, using a fake name, at a bookstore across town, on a night I told you I was at Priya's.*

There wasn't a version of that sentence that fit into our house. Our house ran on specific, useful information: who was covering the counter, whether the pancit noodles needed restocking, what time Papa's call was on Sunday, whether Nico had turned in his reading log. It did not have a slot open for *here is a thing I made that isn't for anyone, that doesn't help anyone, that I do purely because if I don't I feel like I'm going to come apart at a seam nobody can see.*

I slid the notebook out just far enough to check the corner of the page I'd been working on that morning, before school, in the four minutes I'd carved out while my cereal went soft.

*the diner keeps the lights on
so nobody asks
what's burning underneath—*

That was as far as I'd gotten. I didn't love it yet. It was doing the thing my early drafts always did, being too on the nose about the metaphor, wearing its meaning on the outside like a coat instead of trusting the reader to feel the cold. I'd fix it. I had three days before Thursday, and Thursday was the only night of the week that was mine, and I intended to have something ready.

"Bea." My mother's voice, from the kitchen doorway, and I closed the notebook and had it back under the counter and my hand on a rag before she'd finished saying my name, which was its own kind of muscle memory, the same reflex that made me duck when a ball came at my face. "Did you take the trash out?"

"Doing it now."

"And your homework?"

"Also doing that. Later. Tonight." This was not, strictly, a lie, in the sense that I did intend to do my homework at some point before I went to sleep, in the same way it wasn't strictly a lie every single day of my life, just an optimistic sequencing of events that generally worked out.

My mother came and stood at the end of the counter, wiping her hands on the towel that lived permanently over her shoulder like a second collarbone. She looked tired in the specific way she looked tired at 9:15 on a Tuesday, which was different from how she looked tired on a Friday, which was different again from how she looked the week before rent, which I'd learned to read the way other kids probably read their parents' moods off a tone of voice. My mother's exhaustion had dialects.

"Twenty years," she said, apropos of nothing, looking around the diner like she was seeing it for the first time, or maybe the four thousandth. "Twenty years next month."

"Since you opened?"

"Since your Lolo and I signed the lease." She said it and her mouth did something complicated, pride and grief close enough together that I couldn't always tell them apart on her, since my grandfather had died the year before I was born and existed for me only as a photo behind the register and a set of stories that got told at specific, predictable moments — his birthday, the diner's anniversary, any time the flat-top acted up, because apparently he used to talk to it. "We should do something. For the twenty years."

"Like what?"

"I don't know yet." She looked at me, and there was something bright behind her tiredness for a second, an idea catching. "Something big. Bring people in. Show them we're still here."

I nodded, because that was what you did with my mother's ideas when they were still small and bright like that — you nodded, and you let them get bigger, and you found out later what exactly you'd signed up for. It was a system that had worked fine for sixteen years.

"Go home," she said then, meaning *not yet, but soon,* meaning she still had another hour of the books and the prep for tomorrow and the particular ritual by which she made sure the diner was ready to be a diner again in nine hours. "Take your Lola. Make sure Nico's asleep, not just in bed pretending."

"He does the flashlight thing?"

"He does the flashlight thing." She almost smiled. "Go."

I got Lola, who did not want to leave the ledger but came anyway, grumbling in Tagalog about how the numbers would forget themselves overnight if she didn't finish, and we walked the six blocks home in the specific cold that Port Alden got in September, the kind that came off the water and got into your sleeves. Lola held my arm the way she always did, not because she needed to, she'd tell you, but because the sidewalk was uneven and somebody had to keep an old woman from breaking her hip, might as well be her favorite grandchild.

"I'm your only grandchild who lives here," I said.

"That's what makes you the favorite. Very simple math." She squeezed my arm. "You're quiet tonight."

"I'm quiet every night."

"No," she said, and there was something in how she said it, careful, like she was setting a plate down without letting it clink against another one. "Not like this."

I didn't answer that, because I didn't have an answer that would have fit into the six blocks we had left, and because some nights the kindest thing anyone did for me was let a silence stay a silence. Lola let it stay. We walked the rest of the way with just our footsteps and the sound of the harbor somewhere behind us, and in my backpack, against my spine the whole way home, the notebook rode along like something I was smuggling across a border only I could see.
