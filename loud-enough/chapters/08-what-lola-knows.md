# Chapter Eight: What Lola Knows

I hid the folder in the bottom of my backpack, under the notebook, under a broken calculator I kept meaning to throw away, in the specific archaeology of a bag that had become, without my quite planning it, a filing system for the parts of my life that couldn't survive daylight at home.

For three days I didn't do anything with it. I carried the media release form to school and back like a small dense planet with its own gravity, felt it in the bag on my shoulder at the register, at the flat-top, at the bus stop, felt it the way you feel a tooth that's starting to ache before it's ready to actually hurt.

I told Eli about the win on the Thursday after, and he'd hugged me right there behind the Marlow's counter, a fast, warm, completely unselfconscious thing that startled us both a little, and then he'd asked, carefully, "Have you told your mom yet," and I'd said not yet, and he'd nodded and not pushed, but I'd caught something pass behind his eyes, a flicker of the same worry Priya kept not quite voicing, the two of them independently arriving at the same quiet concern: that I was going to let this go the way I let everything private in my life go, hoarded and unspent, a coin I was too afraid to actually cash in.

Lola found me on the fourth day, not because I was careless — I want that on the record, even now, even having lived through everything that came after — but because Lola had spent seventy-one years learning to read a house the way other people read weather, and I had apparently been broadcasting a storm front for weeks without knowing it.

It was a Tuesday, slow afternoon, the dead stretch between lunch and the early dinner crowd when the diner emptied out enough that Lola liked to sit in the back booth with her tea and watch the door, less because she expected anyone and more, I think, because watching the door was a form of rest for her, the diner equivalent of staring at the ocean.

"Sit," she said, when I came through with a rag to wipe down tables nobody had used in an hour, which was Lola-speak for *I have decided this is happening now,* and I sat, because you did not say no to Lola when she'd decided something, it was like arguing with the tide.

She didn't say anything for a while. She drank her tea. She looked at the door. Sampaguita's window box was visible past her shoulder, the white flowers she fussed over every morning even though Port Alden's climate fought her on it every single season, the sampaguita never quite as full or fragrant here as she swore it had been back home, in Cebu, in a garden she described sometimes with a wistfulness that made me understand, even as a kid, that some things you carry with you don't survive the trip intact, they just survive, which isn't the same thing, but is still something.

"You have been happy," Lola said finally, in the tone of an accusation, "and sad, at the same time. For weeks now. I have been trying to figure out how a person does that."

"I don't know what you mean."

"Anak." She looked at me over her tea, and there was nothing sharp in her face, which somehow made it worse, easier to fold under than if she'd come at me hard. "I changed your diapers. I know your face better than you know your face. You have had a secret since you were fourteen years old, and I let you have it, because everybody is allowed one secret, especially at fourteen, especially in a house like this one, where everybody's business is everybody's business except when I decide it isn't." She said it simply, no drama in it, and I felt something in my chest go very still, the specific stillness of a thing that has just realized it's been seen for far longer than it thought. "But you have a new secret now. On top of the old one. And this one is bigger, I think, because the old one made you happy quietly, and the new one is making you happy loudly, in a way you keep having to stuff back down before your mother sees it."

I didn't say anything. I couldn't, for a second, find the part of myself that knew how to lie to Lola, which was strange, because I'd been lying to everyone in this building in some fashion for two years and had gotten, I'd thought, quite good at it.

"How long have you known?" I asked instead, which wasn't a denial, and Lola, who had been waiting patiently for exactly this moment for what I would later understand was closer to a year than a month, allowed herself something that was almost a smile.

"Known what? You haven't told me anything yet."

"Lola."

"I found a poem," she said, and reached into the pocket of her apron, and pulled out a folded piece of paper I recognized with a jolt of pure vertigo — a page from my notebook, an early draft, one I'd thought I'd lost months ago, torn out because I hated an early version of a line and thrown away, except apparently not thrown away far enough, apparently retrieved from wherever it had ended up and kept, all this time, in my grandmother's apron pocket like a rosary. "In the recycling, back in the spring. I almost didn't read it. Then I read the first line and I couldn't stop." She unfolded it, though she didn't need to, I could see she had it essentially memorized. "*The diner keeps the lights on so nobody asks what's burning underneath.* You wrote that?"

I nodded. My throat had done something complicated and tight.

"It's a good line," Lola said. "Better than good. I sat with that piece of paper for a long time, trying to figure out what to do with it. Whether to tell your mother." She folded it back up, carefully, along the same worn creases, and I understood she had unfolded and refolded this specific piece of paper many, many times over many months. "I decided not to. Because it wasn't finished — I could tell, the way it stopped in the middle — and I thought, if she's writing something like this and hiding it, then it's not ready to be found yet, and a thing that's not ready to be found should get to stay hidden a little longer. That was my decision. I don't know if it was the right one."

"Lola—"

"Let me finish." She held up a hand, gentle but absolute. "I have known for months there was a notebook. I did not know there was a Thursday night, or a bookstore, or whatever the new thing is that's been sitting in your backpack for two weeks weighing you down like a brick." Her eyes went to my bag, hung on the hook by the booth, and I realized with a lurch that she'd clocked the new density of it days ago, the folder's added weight, the way I'd started holding the strap differently. "I am asking you to tell me now. Not because I need to know everything. Because I think you are about to explode, quietly, the way your mother explodes, which is to say not at all, until one day it is very much at all, and I would rather catch this before it gets to that."

So I told her. Not everything — I didn't have the courage yet to say Eli's name out loud, that felt like its own separate door — but the notebook, Marlow's, two years of Thursdays under the name B, the regional heat, the folder in my bag with a media release form that needed a parent's signature, the finals in the state capital on November seventh.

Lola listened without interrupting, which was, for Lola, a genuine feat of restraint, her tea going cold in front of her, her face doing very little, which I'd learned over sixteen years meant she was doing a great deal of thinking behind it.

"You won," she said, when I finished. Not a question.

"I won the regional. The finals are different, it's the whole state, I don't know if—"

"You won," Lola said again, cutting through my hedging with the specific flatness she used when she wanted a fact to be heard as a fact and not negotiated with. "Beatrice. You won a poetry competition, in front of strangers, under your own name, and you have been carrying that around for four days like it was something to apologize for."

"I don't know how to tell Mom."

"I know you don't." Lola reached across the table and took my hand, her grip dry and strong and exactly the same as it had been my whole life, unchanged since I was small enough to need help crossing the street. "That is the actual problem, anak. Not the notebook. Not the bookstore. Not even your mother, who I think would surprise you, if you let her. The problem is that you have decided, somewhere along the way, that the only way to be loved in this family is to never need anything from it. And that is not true. That has never once been true. It is just the story you told yourself because it was easier than finding out."

I sat with that in the empty diner, the light coming gold and low through the front windows, the sampaguita in the window box nodding faintly in a draft from the door, and I did not cry, though I came close, close enough that Lola squeezed my hand once, hard, and let go, and said, in her regular voice, like she hadn't just handed me something that would take me weeks to fully unpack:

"Now. Bring me more tea. And think about how you are going to tell your mother, because I am not going to do it for you, but I will stand next to you when you do."
