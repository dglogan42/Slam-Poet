# Chapter Thirteen: Both Rooms

The anniversary came first, and it was, against every anxious prediction my mother had made about it for a month, exactly the night she'd been picturing when she slid that folder across the booth table back in September.

Sampaguita Diner on October twenty-fourth was louder and fuller than I had ever seen it in sixteen years of living half my life inside its walls. My mother had strung new lights alongside Lola's old Christmas ones, and somebody's cousin really did play guitar, soft and unobtrusive in the corner by the window box, and the old dishes Lola had spent weeks teaching my mother's hands to make — a pork dish with a name I'd only ever heard in family stories, a rice cake recipe that made Lola cry a little when she tasted my mother's first attempt and pronounced it, finally, correct — sold out twice and had to be remade mid-service.

The newspaper photographer came and took a photo that ran the following Thursday of my mother and Lola standing together in front of the register, my grandfather's photo just visible over their shoulders, and underneath it a caption that used the word "beloved," which made Lola scoff and secretly, I noticed, cut out and tape inside her ledger anyway.

Nico's balloons were, by his own six-year-old-adjacent assessment, a triumph. I ran the front of house for six straight hours without a single mistake in the reservation book, and somewhere around nine o'clock, in the middle of the rush, I looked up from the register and caught my mother watching me across the room — not checking on me, not making sure I was handling it, just watching, the way you watch something you're proud of, and she caught me catching her and didn't look away, just smiled, tired and open, and went back to the flat-top.

I didn't perform anything that night. It wasn't the moment for it, and I understood that without anyone needing to say it — this night belonged to the diner, to twenty years, to my grandfather's photo finally getting the crowd he deserved. My poem, if I had one, was still three weeks off, waiting in a different room.

The two weeks between the anniversary and the finals passed in the specific compressed way time does when you're both dreading and wanting something equally hard. I rehearsed in my room at night, notebook open, the three poems I'd chosen — the diner poem, the frozen-screen poem about my father, and a new one, finished only four days before, that I hadn't shown anyone yet, not even Priya, not even Eli, who I'd finally, properly introduced to my mother on a Thursday when he walked me home from Marlow's and she happened to be locking up the diner at the same time, an introduction that had gone better than I'd let myself hope, mostly because my mother, it turned out, liked anyone who could correctly identify all four of the old dishes on the anniversary menu by smell alone, which Eli, having eaten there twice already on my recommendation, could.

November seventh arrived cold and bright, the specific hard blue Port Alden sky got in early winter, no clouds, everything sharp-edged.

Priya picked me up at 6:45 in the Corolla, thermos of coffee in the cup holder, the **GO B** sign — retired from active service since regionals but apparently never fully decommissioned — riding in the back seat "just in case." My mother came out onto the front step in her robe to hug me before I left, longer than she needed to, and said, into my hair, "Call me the second you're done performing. I don't care what round it is."

"I will."

"And Bea." She held me at arm's length, hands on my shoulders, her face doing the thing it had started doing more often in the past three weeks, unguarded in a way I was still getting used to. "Whatever happens up there. You already won something, coming home and telling me the truth. I need you to know the contest doesn't get to take that back, either way it goes."

The drive was three hours of highway and gas station coffee and Priya's debate-team playlist, which was mostly instrumental music she swore helped her think and which I found, that morning, weirdly perfect for the specific hum of nerves sitting under my ribs the whole way there.

The venue was a real theater this time, not a library community room — the state capital's old vaudeville house, restored, red velvet seats, a stage with real wings and real lighting, forty-some finalists from regional heats across the entire state milling in the lobby with the particular electric nervousness of people who'd each already won something to get here and now had to find out what that was actually worth.

I performed the diner poem first, in the youth category's opening round, and it landed the way it always landed, the held-breath quiet before applause, and I scored well enough to advance without needing to sweat the number.

Second round, I did the frozen-screen poem about my father, and this time, unlike at regionals, I looked for his face in the audience out of pure old habit before remembering, with a small ache, that he was four thousand miles away on a construction site, that the only way he'd ever hear this poem was through a video I'd promised to send once I found the nerve to record it, and I performed it anyway, to a theater full of strangers, and let the ache become part of the performance instead of something to hide from it.

Third round — finals within the finals, top eight from the youth category, one more poem each — I did the new one. The one nobody had heard yet.

*My mother's mother crossed an ocean
with a flower that wasn't built for here,
planted it anyway in a box outside a diner window,
watered it every morning before the doors opened,
watched it come up smaller than it used to,
paler than it used to,
and bloom anyway, every single year,
stubborn, off-season, half the size it was allowed to be back home—

and I used to think that flower was sad.
A small tragedy in a window box.
Something beautiful, reduced.

I don't think that anymore.

I think my grandmother knew something
I am only now starting to learn:
that a thing doesn't have to bloom
the way it bloomed somewhere else
to still be blooming.
That you can carry your whole self
into the wrong hemisphere, the wrong weather,
a life built for someone else's needs first —
and still, some mornings,
against every reasonable expectation,
open anyway.

My mother gave up a scholarship
to keep a family's lights on.
My grandmother crossed an ocean
and kept a flower alive out of pure stubborn love.
I spent two years hiding my own voice
in a bookstore across town
because I thought a family that runs on being useful
had no room left over for a thing
that helps no one, feeds no one,
balances no one's register —

but I was wrong about the room.
There was room the whole time.
I was just the one who hadn't
watered myself yet,
hadn't planted myself
anywhere real,
hadn't said out loud,
in my own name,
under my own light:

I am also allowed to bloom
a little strange, a little late,
in weather nobody built for me,
and call it,
finally,
enough.*

The theater did the held-breath thing longer than it had for either of my other two poems, long enough that I actually felt a flicker of fear that I'd lost the room entirely — and then the applause came up like a wave breaking, and a woman three rows back who I didn't know was on her feet before the clapping even properly started, and I stood there under the real stage lighting of the state capital's old vaudeville house and let myself, for one entire unguarded minute, believe that I had just done something that mattered, on purpose, in my own name, with nobody left to hide it from.

I placed second in the state. Not first — first went to a boy from the eastern part of the state who did a piece about his brother's deployment that undid the entire room, myself included, and I meant it with my whole chest when I found him afterward and told him so. Second place came with a smaller cash prize than first, and a guaranteed spot in the anthology, and, more than either of those things, the simple fact of a program with my actual name printed in it, **Beatrice Santos — 2nd Place, Youth Division**, a piece of paper I would keep for the rest of my life.

I called my mother from the theater lobby, both hands shaking, Priya bouncing on her toes beside me holding the **GO B** sign upside down because she'd forgotten, in her excitement, which way was correct.

"Second," I said, before she could even finish saying hello. "Mom, I got second in the whole state."

The sound she made was not a word. It was somewhere past a word, something that came straight from the part of a person that doesn't bother translating joy into language first, and I heard, behind her, Lola's voice asking what happened, and my mother relaying it, and then Lola's voice again, closer to the phone now, saying something in rapid delighted Tagalog I only caught pieces of, and underneath both of them, faintly, the sound of the diner at full dinner rush, plates and voices and someone's laugh, the whole loud, lit-up, twenty-year-old room going on without me for one more hour, waiting for me to come fill the seat that had always, it turned out, been kept for me.

"Get in the car," my mother said, laughing and crying at the same time in a way I could hear even through the phone. "Get in the car right now. Dinner rush needs you. And Bea — bring the program. I want to hang it up by the register, right next to your Lolo's photo, where everyone who walks through that door can see it."

Priya drove fast and a little illegally the whole way back, both of us singing badly to the radio somewhere around hour two, the cold blue November light fading into an ordinary Port Alden dusk, and I got back to Sampaguita Diner at 7:40 that night, walked in through the door with the bell that had rung so many times it sounded like a fact of physics, and found the whole family exactly where I'd left them that morning — except now, for the first time in my whole life, they were also waiting for me, specifically, for the version of me that had a name of her own on a piece of paper, and a poem in her chest that had finally, finally, been allowed to be loud enough.
