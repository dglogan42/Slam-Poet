# Chapter Ten: What Elena Gave Up

Lola came home on Saturday, three days after the fall, with a folder of her own — discharge papers, a follow-up appointment card, a list of medications in print too small for her to read without her glasses, which she waved off when my mother tried to fuss over it, saying, "I have read smaller. I have read the fine print on a lease. This is nothing."

She was, by every visible measure, herself again. The slackness was gone entirely; her words came out at their normal rapid-fire clip, English short and exact, Tagalog long and funny. But something in the house had shifted anyway, the way a room shifts after furniture's been moved even if you put it back in exactly the same place — you can't always see what's different, but you feel it every time you walk through the doorway.

My mother had missed two full days at the diner, the first time in as long as I could remember that Sampaguita had run dinner service without her actually present, on-site, watching the flat-top like it might misbehave if she looked away. I'd run the front with a part-time cook named Rosa my mother trusted enough to call in for emergencies, and I'd been proud of how well it had gone, and also viscerally aware, the whole time, of how much of my mother's life was built on a structure that had exactly zero slack in it — one grandmother's dizzy spell and the entire operation had needed every single family member to bend simultaneously just to keep from breaking.

Saturday night, after Lola was settled and Nico was asleep and the diner had closed for the one night my mother allowed herself off in a decade, she sat at our kitchen table with a cup of tea she wasn't drinking, and I sat across from her, and for once neither of us had a task in front of us, no register, no ledger, no rush to get to. Just two tired people and a quiet kitchen.

"I keep thinking about the hospital waiting room," she said, not looking at me, turning the mug slowly on the table the way I turned my coffee cup at Marlow's, the same stalling habit apparently passed down a whole generation before I'd thought to notice I had it. "Sitting there for four hours not knowing anything. You have a lot of time to think in a room like that."

"What were you thinking about?"

"Nursing school." She said it plainly, like it should have meant something to me, and when my face must have shown that it didn't, she looked up, surprised, and then something crossed her face that was almost a laugh, rueful. "I never told you that story."

"No."

"I got a scholarship. Full ride, nursing program, up at the state university, the year I turned nineteen." She said it evenly, like a fact she'd recited before, maybe only ever to herself. "I wanted to be a nurse since I was a girl in Cebu. I used to practice on my dolls — bandages, little splints made of chopsticks. Your Lola thought it was very funny."

"What happened?"

"Your Lolo and Lola were opening the restaurant that same year. Not this one — the first one, back home, before we all came here. And the year the scholarship came through was the same year they needed help most, the opening year, the year everything either survives or doesn't." She turned the mug another quarter turn. "So I deferred it. Just for a year, I told myself. Just to get them through the opening. And then the year after that, my father — your Lolo's father, your other Lolo, who you never met — he got sick, and there was no one else to run the front of house while everyone else took turns at his bedside. So I deferred again."

I tried to picture it — a restaurant I'd never seen except in a handful of photographs gone soft and orange with age, a narrow storefront on a street in Cebu my mother described sometimes in fragments, the ceiling fans that never quite kept up with the heat, my mother at nineteen carrying plates between tables with a nursing textbook, she told me once, hidden under the counter for the slow hours that never actually came. I had grown up thinking of that restaurant as a kind of prologue, a story that existed only to explain how we'd ended up here, at Sampaguita, on this particular stretch of the Port Alden waterfront. It had never once occurred to me that it might also be the place where my mother's other life — the one with bandages and real patients and a white coat she never got to wear — had quietly closed its door and stayed closed.

"Did you ever go back? To see if you could finish it later, once things settled?"

"There is no 'once things settled,' anak. That's the first thing I want you to understand, because I think it's the thing you're still waiting for, without knowing it — some future Tuesday when the diner runs itself and everyone's healthy and there's finally room. That Tuesday doesn't come. You have to make the room yourself, in the middle of everything still being complicated, or you wait your whole life for a stillness that was never going to arrive on its own."

"Mom."

"By the third year, the scholarship program had a rule. You could only defer twice. So it went to someone else." She said this last part with no visible bitterness at all, which somehow made it land harder than if she'd cried, a fact so old and so worn smooth by handling that it had stopped being a wound and become just a shape she carried, the way Lola carried the sampaguita cuttings across an ocean and a hemisphere of wrong weather. "I met your father two years after that. We opened Sampaguita five years after that. I don't regret my life, Bea. I want to say that first, very clearly, because I can already see your face deciding this is a sad story, and it isn't, not entirely."

"It sounds a little sad."

"It is a little sad," she allowed. "Both things can be true. I don't regret you, or your brother, or this diner, or the twenty years I've spent standing at that flat-top. And also, some nights, very late, when nobody is around to see it, I think about what it would have felt like to put a real bandage on a real person and know I'd helped them the way I used to imagine, as a girl with dolls and chopsticks. I let that thought have exactly a few minutes, most nights, and then I put it away, because there isn't room in my day for more than a few minutes of it, and I've made my peace with that." She finally looked straight at me, and I understood, with a lurch, that this whole conversation had been leading somewhere very deliberately, that my mother did not do anything, ever, without an actual destination in mind. "I'm not telling you this so you'll feel sorry for me. I'm telling you because I sat in that waiting room for four hours, not knowing if my own mother was going to be all right, and the whole time, underneath the fear, there was this other thought I couldn't shake, and it was about you."

"Me?"

"You have a face, Bea, that does the thing my face used to do. The thing I taught myself, I think, without meaning to — deciding what you want doesn't matter before anyone even gets the chance to ask you what it is." She reached across the table, the same gesture Lola had made three days earlier, and took my hand. "I found the folder."

The floor of my stomach dropped out entirely. "What folder?"

"The one you've been carrying in your backpack for two weeks like it's made of lead. I wasn't snooping, I promise you that — I was looking for a pen in the front pocket Thursday night, before all of this happened, and I saw the state seal on it, and I didn't open it, I want you to know that, I put it right back exactly where it was. But I saw enough of the top page to know it wasn't school." She squeezed my hand once, gently, nothing like an accusation in it. "I've been waiting to see if you'd tell me yourself. I decided, in that hospital, that if we got through this — if my mother was all right — the very first thing I was going to do when things were calm enough was ask you to."

I sat there with my hand in my mother's hand, in a kitchen that had, over the course of one terrible week, become the site of more honest conversation than the previous two years combined, and I understood that the moment I had been so carefully, elaborately avoiding for so long had simply arrived on its own timeline after all, exactly like I'd feared it would, except it didn't feel like the ambush I'd always pictured. It felt like a door someone had been holding open, patiently, waiting for me to notice it wasn't locked.

"There's a notebook," I said, and started, finally, at the beginning.
