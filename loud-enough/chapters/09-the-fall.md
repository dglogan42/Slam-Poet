# Chapter Nine: The Fall

I was going to tell my mother that Friday. I had decided this the way I decided most hard things, by picking a date far enough away to feel safe and close enough to feel real, and Friday had felt, on Tuesday night when Lola squeezed my hand and told me to think about it, like a reasonable amount of runway.

I never got the chance, because Thursday the diner had its worst rush in a year, and Lola fell.

Thursday, of all nights. My night, technically, though I hadn't made it to Marlow's — the diner had called in Rosa sick with a cold that turned out to be a stomach bug by four in the afternoon, and my mother, unwilling to run a health-code risk during a full house, had sent her home and asked me, apologetically, if I'd mind staying to help instead of going to Priya's, and I'd said of course, easily, the lie about Priya's not even needing to be spoken out loud that particular week, and some part of me — I would think about this a great deal in the weeks afterward — had actually been relieved, because it meant one fewer Thursday I had to hold two names in my head at once. The dining room had been full by six, the specific chaotic fullness that happened maybe once a month, when a birthday party and the bowling league and a table of tourists off the coastal highway all converged on the same ninety minutes, orders stacking up faster than the flat-top could turn them, my mother moving between stations with the compressed, silent focus she got during a real rush, no wasted words, no wasted motion, Lola picking up the overflow the way she always did on nights like this, moving between tables with a competence that made her look, for whole stretches of the evening, twenty years younger than she was.

It was nothing dramatic to look at, that was the strange, terrible thing about it — no crash, no dropped tray, none of the things you'd brace for if you pictured an emergency happening in a diner at 6:40 on a Thursday night. She was carrying two plates of pancit to the corner booth, the one that always sat six regulars who came in as a bowling league even in the off-season, and she just — stopped. Mid-step, plates still level, and her face did something I had never seen it do before, a kind of slackness on the left side, like a hand had reached out and smudged half of her expression while the right half stayed exactly, eerily itself.

"Lola?"

She didn't answer right away. The plates tipped — not dropped, tipped, slow, like she was setting them down somewhere that wasn't quite the table — and I was moving before I'd finished registering what I was seeing, catching one plate, catching her arm, and her knees went soft under her in a way that had nothing to do with age and everything to do with something happening inside her that neither of us had a name for yet.

"Mom!" I don't remember deciding to scream it. It came out of me the way the poems came out, from somewhere below the part of my brain that made choices. "Mom, something's wrong with Lola!"

What happened next happened in the specific fractured, non-linear way real emergencies happen, nothing like the smooth continuous scenes I'd write later if I ever tried to put this in a poem — my mother was there so fast I never actually saw her cross the room, just found her already kneeling where I'd lowered Lola into a chair, my mother's hands on Lola's face, saying her name, saying "Ma, Ma, look at me," in a voice I had never once heard her use, high and cracked and entirely unlike the register-counting, twice-checked voice she used for everything else in her life.

Lola's mouth moved. Half of it moved. The words that came out were wrong in a way I felt in my own stomach — not wrong like a stroke in a movie, not slurred beyond recognition, just faintly off, syllables landing a half-second behind where they should have, a woman who had spoken two languages fluently for seventy-one years suddenly sounding, for a handful of terrifying seconds, like she was translating everything from somewhere very far away.

"Call 911," my mother said, and I already had my phone out, already dialing, my hands doing the thing hands do in an emergency, which is somehow both shake uncontrollably and perform exactly the sequence of numbers required, both at once, a body running two separate operating systems.

Nico had come out from the back booth where he did homework during dinner rush, and stood frozen in the kitchen doorway with a look on his face I would carry around guiltily for a long time afterward, because nobody thought, in the chaos, to get him out of the room, to spare him watching his grandmother's face go slack in a diner booth, and I would understand later that this was its own kind of failure, the exact kind my whole family specialized in — so consumed by the emergency directly in front of us that we forgot, for several crucial minutes, that there was a ten-year-old standing eight feet away absorbing every second of it.

The ambulance took eleven minutes, which is nothing, which is a genuinely excellent response time for a city like Port Alden, and which also felt, kneeling on the floor of our own diner holding my grandmother's hand while my mother kept saying her name over and over like a rope she was using to pull her back from somewhere, like the longest eleven minutes that had ever existed in the history of time.

By the time the paramedics got her onto the gurney, the slackness in her face had already started to ease, her words coming back into alignment, and one of the paramedics — a woman with kind, tired eyes who clearly did this exact reassurance forty times a week — told my mother, in the calm practiced voice of someone managing a family's terror, "This looks like it might be a TIA. A mini-stroke. The good news is the symptoms are already resolving, that's actually a strong sign, but she needs a full workup at the hospital, and I want you to come with us."

My mother went in the ambulance. I stayed behind with Nico and the diner, half-full of regulars who had gone completely silent, the bowling league still sitting in the corner booth with their pancit going cold, nobody quite sure whether they should leave or stay or help, until Mr. Delgado — Mr. Delgado, of all people, who I'd been slightly rolling my eyes at for years over his Tuesday order — stood up and started, quietly, competently, helping me clear the booths and get people their checks and turn the sign to closed, a small mercy from a man I had never once thought to look at closely enough to know he had this in him.

I got Nico home. I sat with him on the couch and let him talk in circles about what he'd seen, the specific way kids process fear by narrating it over and over until the narrating wears the fear down to something smaller, and I held him even though he was ten and would normally have squirmed away from being held, and neither of us said out loud the thing we were both thinking, which was: *is she going to die,* because saying it out loud felt like it might make it true.

My mother texted updates in fragments. *At hospital. Waiting on scan.* Then, forty minutes later, *scan looks ok so far. still testing.* Then, an hour after that, close to eleven, *she's talking normal again. they think it really was just a TIA. staying overnight to be safe.*

Priya called somewhere in the middle of that stretch, having heard secondhand through the Port Alden group chat that always seemed to know things before they were official, and I told her everything in a flat, fast voice that didn't sound like my own, and she didn't try to make it better, didn't offer any of the things people offer when they don't know what else to do — she just stayed on the phone with me, mostly silent, occasionally saying my name like a checkpoint, until Nico finally fell asleep against my shoulder on the couch and I had to whisper that I needed to go. Eli texted once, a single line — *heard from Priya. no need to answer this, just wanted you to know I'm thinking about you* — and I stared at it for a long time without writing anything back, not because I didn't want to, but because I didn't have any words left over that night that weren't already spoken for.

I didn't sleep. I lay in bed with my phone on my chest and thought about the folder in my backpack, the media release form, the finals in November, the conversation I'd planned to have on Friday, and felt something close to shame at how small and self-involved all of it suddenly seemed, a poetry competition, a secret bookstore, a boy I liked — measured against my grandmother's face going slack over two plates of pancit, against the eleven minutes I would remember for the rest of my life as the longest of my whole existence.

I thought: *I am never going to bring this up now.* Not Friday, not this month, maybe not ever, because how could I possibly walk into a hospital room and tell my exhausted, frightened mother that on top of everything else, her daughter had a secret life she'd been keeping for two years and a competition three states worth of driving away that she wanted permission to go to — how could that possibly be a conversation that had room to exist in a family that had just watched its own foundation flicker, even briefly, even survivably.

The finals folder went back to the bottom of my backpack. I zipped it in like something dangerous, something I no longer had the right to want.

What I didn't understand yet, lying awake that night listening to Nico's uneven breathing through the wall, was that the fall hadn't ended my story. It had started the part of it where I finally had to learn the actual difference between the two sentences I'd been carrying around for months without knowing how close they really were to each other: *somebody has to,* and *I want something too.*

I didn't know yet that my grandmother, half-conscious in a hospital bed with wires taped to her chest, was going to be the one to teach me that difference — not by telling me, the way Lola usually taught things, but by doing something I never in a hundred years would have predicted from her, three days later, the moment she was strong enough to hold a phone.
