# Chapter Fourteen: Open Mic

Three weeks after the finals, on an ordinary Thursday that had no anniversary, no competition, no folder with a state seal riding in my backpack, I signed up at Marlow's under my own name for the first time in two years.

I almost wrote B out of habit. My hand actually started the letter, the familiar single downstroke, before I caught myself, crossed it out, and wrote **Beatrice Santos** on the clipboard in full, the way you'd sign a lease, and handed the clipboard back to Odette, who read it, looked up at me over her reading glasses, and said, without a trace of surprise, "About time," in the exact tone she'd used for two years to introduce Gary's dog poem, like this, too, was simply a fact of the room now, unremarkable, correct.

My family took up the whole second row.

My mother had closed the diner an hour early, a genuinely unprecedented act in the twenty-year history of Sampaguita, handwritten sign taped to the door — **CLOSED TONIGHT, FAMILY EVENT** — that Mr. Delgado would later tell me he'd photographed on his phone because he wanted proof, someday, that it had actually happened. Lola sat on the aisle in a cardigan I hadn't seen her wear before, having apparently decided the occasion called for it, her rosary looped twice around her wrist the way it always was, touching it now and then not because a number wasn't adding up but because, I think, it was simply a habit of her hands when she was moved by something and didn't have another way to hold the feeling. Nico sat between Lola and my mother with a granola bar he'd smuggled in against Odette's mild, unenforced no-outside-food policy, and a homemade sign he'd made without anyone's help, block letters in marker: **MY SISTER IS COOL.**

Even my father was there, in the only way he could be — propped on the tablet in the seat Priya had saved for exactly this purpose, angled so he had a clear view of the little stage, sitting up at 3am Dubai time in his dormitory bunk, having asked my mother three separate times over the preceding week to confirm the exact time difference so he wouldn't miss it. I'd see his face afterward, replayed later from Priya's video of the whole thing, and understand that he'd watched the entire three minutes without blinking, the particular stillness of a man finally getting to witness, in real time instead of secondhand, exactly who his daughter had quietly become while he was thirty stories up in someone else's country, keeping the lights on back home.

Eli was behind the counter, same as always, making someone's latte with the same bomb-disposal concentration, except every few seconds his eyes came back to me anyway, unable to help it, and when our eyes caught he didn't look away, just held it, steady, the way he'd been doing since the night he told me eleven times, I've heard you eleven times, and I think you'd regret not finding out.

I read the flower poem. The one from the finals stage, the one about Lola's sampaguita and my mother's scholarship and my own two years hiding a whole self in a bookstore across town — except I'd revised the ending in the three weeks since, added six new lines that hadn't existed yet the day I stood in that vaudeville house, because some poems, I was learning, weren't finished the moment you performed them best. They kept growing, the way the flower did, a little more each season.

*I used to think being loved
meant being useful.
I know now it just means
being seen —
and somebody, finally,
staying to watch
the whole bloom,
not just the parts
that were easy to water.*

The applause, when it came, was the loudest Marlow's had ever been on a Thursday night, forty-some regulars and a family of four and one boy behind the counter who'd stopped making the latte entirely to clap with both hands, foam cup abandoned on the counter, and underneath the applause, unmistakable even through a tablet's tinny little speaker, my father's voice, four thousand miles and one very bad connection away, saying something in Tagalog I didn't need to translate to understand, because some things you just feel the shape of, the way you feel the shape of a word before you've fully learned the language it belongs to.

Afterward, Lola caught my hand on the way back to my seat, the same grip she'd used my whole life to keep me from stumbling on an uneven sidewalk, and pulled me down to her level, and said, quiet, just for me, "I kept that page in my apron for a year because I thought it wasn't finished yet. I was wrong. It was finished the whole time. It just needed you to finally believe it was allowed to be read."

The rest of the night went on around me the way Thursday nights at Marlow's always had — Gary did the dog poem, same as always, and I clapped for it with the same conviction I always had, because it was still true and the dog was still, apparently, a genuinely remarkable animal. The two Port Alden kids from the hallway went up, and this time, when I caught their eyes afterward, none of us pretended not to recognize each other, the old etiquette quietly retired now that I'd retired it myself first.

Priya found me by the door at the end of the night, the retired **GO B** sign finally, permanently retired, left folded in the back seat of the Corolla where it would probably live for years. "I've been waiting two years to see that," she said, and didn't try to make it into more of a thing than it needed to be, just bumped her shoulder against mine and let it sit there, satisfied, a debt she considered fully paid.

Eli caught me outside, under the string of bulb lights Marlow's kept up year-round over its side entrance, his shift not quite over, an apron still tied around his waist, and didn't say anything about the poem at first, just looked at me for a second the way he had that very first Thursday, like he was doing careful, deliberate math.

"Eleven times doesn't count anymore," he said finally. "I lost count somewhere around the finals video Priya sent me. I think we're well past keeping score."

"Is that a compliment?"

"I know this one," he said, and this time he didn't make me wait for the answer. "Yes."

I sat back down between my grandmother and my brother's granola bar wrapper, in the second row of a bookstore that had spent two years being the only room in my life with no consequences, and understood that the room had changed permanently now, that consequences had walked in through the door with the bell that had rung so many times it sounded like a fact of physics, and that this — somehow, against every fear I'd carried for two years — was the good version of that change, not a door closing on the private, costless place I'd built for myself, but a door widening, enough for everyone I loved to finally fit through it too, without either room having to disappear to make space for the other.

My notebook still lived in the bottom of my backpack. It still had a soft spine and fuzzed corners and half-finished lines I hadn't found the shape of yet. But it wasn't a secret anymore, which turned out to be a different thing entirely than I'd spent two years afraid it would be — not smaller for being seen, not less mine for being shared.

Just louder. Finally, simply, all the way through, loud enough.
