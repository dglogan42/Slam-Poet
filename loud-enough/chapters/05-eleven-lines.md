# Chapter Five: Eleven Lines

Eli had started saving me the table by the poetry section, the wobbly one with the leg Odette had fixed with a folded coaster years ago and never gotten around to fixing properly, which meant it wasn't really a table so much as a negotiation you entered into every time you set a coffee down on it.

"You're going to do it," he said, the Thursday after the flyer, sliding into the other chair like it wasn't a question, though it was phrased like one.

"Do what."

"Whatever it is. You've had a face for two weeks like someone handed you a decision and didn't include instructions." He said it lightly, not fishing exactly, more just noticing out loud, which I was learning was a thing he did — Eli narrated what he saw without demanding you confirm or deny it, which somehow made you want to confirm it more than if he'd pushed.

I hadn't told him about the flyer. I hadn't told anyone but Priya, and Priya only because Priya had found it first. But I found myself, sitting at the wobbly table with a coffee going cold between us, wanting to tell him, which was its own small alarm — I'd spent two years perfecting the art of Marlow's as a room with no consequences, and here I was considering adding a consequence to it on purpose.

I thought sometimes about the actual first time I'd noticed him, back in August, restocking the poetry section with the specific inefficiency of someone who kept stopping to read the backs of books he was supposed to be shelving. I'd watched him from my usual chair for a full ten minutes before he ever said a word to me, amused at how bad he was at his own job in a way that seemed less like laziness and more like a kind of reverence — he clearly loved the store more than he cared about the store running efficiently, which struck me, even then, before I knew his name, as a good sign about a person. It had taken him three more Thursdays after that to actually speak to me, and when he finally had, it hadn't been about poetry at all. It had been to tell me, apropos of nothing, that I'd been sitting in the same chair for six weeks straight and he wanted to know if it was assigned to me by the state or if I'd just claimed it through sheer persistence. I'd said persistence. He'd said that tracked.

"There's a slam," I said. "A real one. State thing. Regional heat's at the library, first Saturday of October."

"And?"

"And I don't know if I'm doing it."

"What's stopping you?"

I turned the coffee cup a quarter turn on its coaster, which was a stalling habit I'd apparently had for years without noticing until just now, watching my own hand do it. "It's not anonymous. You register under your name. There's a program. My mom reads the local paper — not, like, obsessively, but she'd see it if it ran a photo, everyone in Port Alden who eats at Sampaguita would see it, and then I'd have to explain two years of Thursdays I told her were Priya's house."

"So tell her before she finds a photo."

"It's not that simple."

"I didn't say it was simple. I said tell her." He wasn't being glib about it — I could see that even then, three weeks into whatever this was, that Eli didn't do glib, he thought before he said things, which was rarer in a seventeen-year-old boy than it should have been. "I just don't understand what the alternative is. You keep doing this forever and never let the two things touch?"

"Maybe."

"That sounds exhausting."

"It's less exhausting than the other option," I said, and heard the edge in my own voice, sharper than I meant it, and he didn't flinch from it, just waited, which somehow made me want to explain instead of retreat. "You don't understand what my house is like. Everything in it runs on being useful. My mom runs the kitchen and the books, my Lola's basically the diner's second brain, my brother's ten and he's already figured out the fastest way to get noticed is to be good at something, and my dad's on the other side of the planet wiring buildings so we can afford the lease. There's no slot in that for 'also I write poems that don't help anybody, and I need four hours on a Saturday, the busiest day, to go do something that's entirely, uselessly for me.'"

Eli was quiet for a second. Around us Marlow's did its Thursday-night noise, someone testing the mic with the same two words everyone always tested it with, Odette laughing at something behind the register.

"Can I tell you something," he said, "and you don't have to agree with it, you can just have it."

"Okay."

"I don't think 'useless' means what you think it means." He said it carefully, like he'd been turning the sentence over before he offered it, checking it for sharp edges. "My mom used to play violin. Like, actually played, conservatory-track, before she had me and my sister. She doesn't anymore, hasn't touched it in maybe six years, and I asked her once why, and she said there wasn't room for it once we existed, we needed her more." He turned his own cup a quarter turn, an echo of my own habit that I don't think he noticed he was doing. "She says it like it's just a fact. Not sad, just — arithmetic. But I've seen the case in the closet. She dusts it. Nobody dusts something they've actually made peace with."

I didn't say anything. I was thinking about my mother's face the night she said *twenty years next month,* the specific bright-and-tired thing that had crossed it, pride and something else close enough together I hadn't been able to name the something else.

"I'm not saying your family's like my mom," Eli said. "I don't know your family. I'm just saying — the poems not helping anybody isn't the same as the poems being useless. Some things you need in a house that aren't about the register balancing." He shrugged, embarrassed suddenly, like he'd said more than he meant to. "Also, for what it's worth, I've heard you read eleven times now. I counted. And I think you'd regret not finding out what happens if a room full of strangers who don't know your name gets the whole poem instead of three minutes of it."

"Eleven times," I repeated. "You counted."

"I have a good memory for things that matter to me," he said, and didn't take it back or make a joke to soften it, just let it sit there in the space between us, true and slightly too big for the wobbly table, and I felt my ears do the warm thing again, the thing they'd started doing around him with alarming regularity.

"Do you write?" I asked, mostly to give my ears somewhere else to send the blood. "You never sign up."

"I write. I don't perform." He said it without any embarrassment, just a fact about himself he'd apparently made peace with a while ago. "I'm not brave like that. I like the version of this where I get to hand somebody a page and walk away before they finish reading it. Watching your face read something is worse than any stage, to me."

"That's a very specific kind of brave, actually. Just a quieter one."

"Maybe." He considered that like it was new information, turning it over. "My sister's the performer in the family. She does theater, real stuff, leads in the school musicals, completely unbothered by three hundred people watching her exist. I used to think there was something wrong with me because I didn't have that. Now I think we just come at the same fear from different directions — she needs the whole room looking at her to feel real, and I need nobody looking at all." He glanced at me, something careful in it. "You're different from both of us. You need the room, but only if it doesn't know your name. That's its own thing. I don't think I've seen it before you."

"Is that a compliment?"

"I don't know yet," he said, honestly, which was somehow more flattering than if he'd just said yes. "Ask me again once I've watched what you do with a whole state's worth of strangers instead of forty people in a bookstore."

I signed up for the regional heat that night, on my phone, in the Marlow's bathroom, hands not quite steady, using my actual name for the first time on anything related to poetry since Ms. Okafor's freshman-year assignment. **Beatrice Santos.** Category: Youth, ages 13–18. I used Priya's address for the confirmation email because I didn't trust my own inbox not to get glanced at during a family laptop moment, which was its own small, ugly acknowledgment of exactly how much I still intended to hide.

I didn't tell Eli I'd done it. I went back out and sat down and let him think the conversation had just been a conversation, and he didn't push, because that was the thing about Eli I was starting to understand at the two-month mark of whatever we were — he offered you the truth and then let you decide, in your own time, what to do with it, without ever standing over your shoulder to check.

Walking to Priya's car after — she'd stuck around reading in the corner, which she did some Thursdays now, having appointed herself unofficial getaway driver and occasional audience — I told her.

"I signed up," I said, and Priya, who was not a person who celebrated quietly, made a sound in the school parking lot loud enough that a guy loading his car two spaces over turned to look.

"Bea."

"Don't make it a thing."

"It's extremely a thing, I'm allowed to make it a thing, this is the thing I've been trying to get you to do for two years—"

"I haven't told my mom."

That stopped her, the way I knew it would, her whole face recalibrating from celebration into something more careful. "Okay," she said. "When are you going to?"

"I don't know. After, maybe. If it goes well."

"And if it doesn't go well?"

"Then there's nothing to tell," I said, which felt, at the time, like a genuinely reasonable plan, a clean piece of logic with no holes in it, the kind of plan a person makes when they haven't yet learned that secrets don't actually wait patiently for you to decide when to reveal them. Secrets have their own timeline. They come out when they're ready, not when you are, and mine — I didn't know it yet, getting into Priya's Corolla that smelled like a highlighter, my actual name freshly attached to a poetry competition for the first time in my life — mine was already, quietly, starting the clock.
