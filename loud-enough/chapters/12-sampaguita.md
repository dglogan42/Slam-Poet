# Chapter Twelve: Sampaguita

Lola was the one who fixed it, though "fixed" isn't quite the right word for what she did, because Lola didn't fix things the way you'd fix a leaking faucet, with a clean solution that made the problem disappear. She fixed things the way you'd fix a torn sleeve — visibly, with a stitch you could still see if you looked, but sound, something that would hold.

She came to my room the next morning, before school, while my mother was already at the diner starting the day's bake, and sat on the edge of my bed without asking, the way she'd sat next to me at the register weeks ago, patting the space beside her, which I took, feeling about nine years old again, my legs pulled up under me the way I used to sit when she told me stories at bedtime.

"Your mother didn't sleep," Lola said. "I heard her in the kitchen at four. Not working. Just sitting."

"Is she still mad at me?"

"She was never mad at you, anak. She was scared, and scared looks exactly like mad if you catch it from the wrong angle, and you two have been catching each other from the wrong angle for years, I think, because you both do the same thing when you're frightened — you go quiet and hard, instead of loud and soft, and neither of you has ever learned how to tell the other one what's actually happening underneath it." Lola shook her head, half exasperated, half fond, the exact expression she wore watching the register drawer refuse to balance on a night she was certain she hadn't made a mistake. "You are so much more alike than either of you wants to admit. That is usually why the fighting is worst between the two of you and not, say, between you and me, or you and your brother. There's no friction in loving people who are different from you. The friction is all in loving the ones who are exactly your own reflection."

"I made her cry."

"You told her the truth. Those aren't the same thing, though I understand why they feel like it right now." She smoothed a wrinkle out of my comforter that didn't need smoothing, a habit-motion, her hands needing something to do while her mouth did the harder work. "I heard the whole fight. The walls in this apartment are not as thick as your mother pretends they are."

"I don't know what to do, Lola. She's not wrong. You almost — " I couldn't finish it, the sentence still too raw three weeks out. "She really does need me. It's not like I made that up to get out of the finals."

"I know she needs you. I have needed you too, for longer than is fair to a girl your age, and I have known that and said nothing about it for longer than I am proud of." Lola's voice had none of its usual brisk certainty in it; this was a softer register, one I heard maybe twice a year, reserved for the truest things she had to say. "But anak, listen to me, because I am only going to say this once and then I am going to make you breakfast and pretend I didn't say anything at all, because that is how our family does its most important conversations, apparently, sideways, over food. Your mother did not almost lose you when I fell in that diner. She almost lost me. And in the middle of being terrified of that, she reached for the only tool she has ever known how to use to keep a family safe, which is work. More work, harder work, everybody pulling together, because that is the only language of love this family has ever really trusted." She took my hand, the second time in three weeks someone in this family had done exactly that gesture, and I understood, sitting there, that it was a language too, a whole vocabulary of love spoken through hands reaching across tables. "It is not the only language there is. It's just the only one she was ever taught."

"So what do I do?"

"You do not choose between the diner and the poem, because that is the wrong question, and your mother asked it wrong too, in her fear, and you both answered it wrong back at each other." Lola stood, smoothing her skirt, businesslike again, the soft register closing back up like a door. "You find the plan that lets you keep both, and you bring it to her already built, instead of asking her permission for something you haven't figured out yet. That is what she understands. Not speeches. Plans."

I thought about that the whole walk to school, and through first period, and through the part of second period where I was supposed to be taking notes on the Missouri Compromise and instead sketched, in the margin of my notebook, the actual shape of the problem: November seventh. Extended anniversary hours. My mother needing hands at the diner, all day, the biggest Saturday of the whole season according to her own excited planning.

I texted Priya at lunch. *Can you drive me to the capital and back on the 7th. Its a big ask I know*

She texted back within a minute: *obviously. already blocked it off in my calendar three weeks ago you idiot, I knew you'd need me eventually*

Which solved exactly one piece of it — I wouldn't need my mother to drive, wouldn't need to burden the family car or gas money or anyone's Saturday but Priya's, who it turned out had been quietly ready for this exact request since before I'd even admitted to myself I might make it. But the diner itself still needed hands, my hands specifically, and I sat with that piece of the puzzle through the rest of the day until it clicked, finally, in AP Bio, staring at a diagram of mitosis for the second time this story, cells splitting into exact, functional copies of themselves.

I couldn't split myself into two functional Beas. But I didn't actually need to be there the *whole* day. The finals schedule, sitting in the folder I'd finally stopped hiding, had registration at nine and awards at six, but my actual performance slot, once I checked, was early — youth category went first, my three rounds finished by early afternoon if I made it through, which meant, if Priya drove fast and the traffic gods were kind, I could be back in Port Alden by seven, maybe seven-thirty. Not for the whole anniversary-season Saturday. But for dinner rush. For the part of the night, my mother had told me once, that actually mattered most, when the whole neighborhood came through the door.

I brought the plan home that night, after closing, when the diner had emptied out and my mother was doing her double-count on the register, the crease still between her eyebrows but her hands steady, the ritual itself maybe the only thing keeping her upright some days.

"I have an idea," I said, and laid it out — Priya driving, the early performance slot, back by dinner rush, missing only the daytime hours, not the whole day, not the thing that mattered most to her. "I know it's not nothing. You'd still be short a person all morning. But I could ask Wren — the girl who came in second at regionals, we've stayed in touch, she works catering some weekends — if she knows anyone who does day shifts for a few extra bucks. I could line it up myself. You wouldn't have to find the coverage. I would."

My mother finished her second count before she answered, which I recognized now as its own kind of ritual, giving herself the length of a task to organize her feelings before she had to speak them out loud. She closed the register drawer.

"You built all this today?"

"During AP Bio, mostly."

That got the smallest laugh out of her, surprised out of her exhaustion, and something in her shoulders came down half an inch. "Twenty years I've been telling myself I don't have room for one more thing in this diner," she said, half to me, half to the room itself, to the string lights and the flat-top and my grandfather's photo watching from behind the register. "And here you are, building me a solution I didn't ask for, on top of everything else you already carry, because that's apparently just who you are, and I don't know if I did that to you or if you were always going to be that person no matter what family you were born into."

"Both, maybe."

"Maybe." She came around the counter and hugged me, tired and real, no performance in it. "Go. November seventh. Do your morning, come back for the night. Bring me a poem sometime. An actual one, not one I have to find in the recycling."

"That was Lola's."

"I know," my mother said. "She told me. Three weeks ago, in the hospital, while we were waiting on your scan results — I mean her scan results, God, I still can't say it right — she told me there was a notebook and a poem so good it made her sit with it for a long time before she decided what to do about it. I didn't say anything to you because I was waiting to see if you'd tell me yourself. Turns out I didn't have to wait very long after all." She smoothed my hair back from my face, an old gesture from when I was small, one she hadn't done in years. "Sampaguita doesn't grow easy here. My mother's been fighting that flower box for twenty years, wrong climate, wrong hemisphere, and every single year it blooms anyway, a little smaller than it would back home, maybe, but it blooms. I think that's just what this family does. Grows things in the wrong weather and refuses to let that be the end of the story."

I didn't have a poem ready right then. But I had one three weeks later, and I would give it to her exactly the way she asked — not hidden in a recycling bin for someone else to find by accident, but placed directly into her hands, on purpose, mine to give.
